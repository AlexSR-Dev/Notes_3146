1. The Actors in a Communication
- The 5 components involved for a computer to send data to another:

1.1) Source/Sender - Host/End System
- The computer/other networked device that originates the information.
• A "host" is a device participating in network communication.
• An "end system" is a device at the edge of the network that runs apps and sends/receives information.

Application: Sending an email, the devices acts as the source.


1.2) Transmitter - Network Interface Card (NIC)
NIC - Provides a computer with a network connetion. A hardware component that connects a (host/end system) to a network.
- Thus, providing the interface through which data is transmitted over a communication medium as signals.
- Moreover, the NIC converts the signals back into data on the receiver side.

Application: An Ethernet NIC sending signals through a network cable.


1.3) Communication Medium
- The physical channel that carries that data in the form of signals from one network interface to another.
EX: Copper Ethernet cabler, which carries electrical signals. OR a Fiber-optic cable, which carries light pulses.

Application: A computer sends info through an Ehernet Cable, the cable carries the signals generated by the transmitting interface.


1.4) Receiver - Network Interface Card (NIC)
- The NIC detects incoming signals and converts them into data that the device can process.

Application: When a comuter receives a message, its network interface receives the incoming transmission.


1.5) Destination or Receiver - Host / End System
- The endpoint for which the information is intended/received.

NOTE: NIC handles the network interface's physical sending and receiving functions.
- The destination devices as the last point info arrives.


Total Application:
Consider sending a photograph from one laptop to another.
1. The sending laptop's application produces the data.
2. Its NIC prepares and transmits the network signals.
3. A wired or wireless medium carries the signals.
4. The receiving laptop's NIC receives the transmission.
5. The receiving laptop's operating system and application process the received data, making the photograph available.

2. The Medium of Communication
- The physical means by which information moves between networked devices.
- Whether the signals travel between a "physical cable" or "open space".


2.1) Wired Networks
- Uses a physical cable to connect network devices.
EX: Coaxial cable (carries electrical signals through a cable), Fiber-optic cable (carries info using pulses of light though opical fibers).


2.2) Wireless Networks
- Tramsits signals without needing physical communication cable between the communicating wireless interfaces.
EX: Wifi, Bluetooth.
Application: Connect to a Wi-Fi network, its wireless NIC transmits and receives radio signals through the air.


NOTE: The medium distinction helps explain why the NIC must match the communication technology, from transmission and receiving them.

3. Network Topologies
- The arrangement of connections among network devices.
: The question - How should multiple computers be connected so that they can communciate efficiently?
- The topology is divided into two board apporaches: "Direct Connectivity" and "Indirect Connectivity"


3.1) Direct Connectivity: Mesh and Bus
3.1.1) Mesh Topology
- Every computer is connected directly to every other computer, with an explicit direct link.
- Direct links are found for "n" computers: (n(n-1)) / 2 , a quadratic growth with more links when devices are added.

Scalability Limitation:
- Adding one new computer requires a direct link to every computer in the mesh. Thus with more devices, more links, more interface
requirements, and greater installation complexity. Thus, better in smaller networks.


3.1.2) Bus Topology
- All computers connect to a common communication line called the bus.
- Thus every device share the same transmission path.

Scalability Limitation:
- With a shared communication resource, their transmission would compete for the shared medium, creating dependency.
Thus, better in smaller networks.



3.2) Why Direct Connectivity Is Usually Unnecessary
- There is not need for every computer to reach every other device directly.
- Instead, a computer can reach another via computers in the middle.
- An intermediate point in that indirect communication becomes practical to build.



3.3) Indirect Connectivity: Ring, Star, and Tree
3.3.1) Ring Topology
- Each computer connects to two neighboring computers, forming a closed loop.
- Thus, communication between two computers may need to trvael through one/more intermediate computers along the ring.
APP: Suppose Computer A needs to communicate with Computer D. If the ring contains A, B, C, and D in that order, the communication could travel along the path: A -> B -> C -> D

Scalability Limitation:
- As the ring grows, a message may need to traverse more intermediate nodes. Thus increasing the path length and possible communication 
delay. Resulting in slowness.


3.3.2) Star Topology
- Each computer connects through an individual link to a central device.
- The central computer called a hub, in which every device that want to communicate must pass through the hub.

Scalability Limitation:
- The central device can become a bottleneck if its capacity cannot meet the traffic it must handle. Thus can disrupt communication,
as devices depend on that sole point.


3.3.3) Extended Star (Tree) Topology
- Connects mutliple star arragnments through a hierarchy of central devices and branches.
Thus, instead of relying on a single central device to connect every computer directly, the network is organized into levels.

EX:
• A main switch connects to two branch switches.
• Each branch switch connects to several computers.
• Computers on the same branch can exchange traffic through their branch switch when the destination is on that branch.
• Traffic between branches must pass through the appropriate upstream connection.

Improves Scalability:
- The hierarchical structure distributes connections and traffic across multiple devices with multiple hubs and branches.
HOWEVER, traffic between branches must pass through the appropriate upstream connection, that may need sufficient capacity.


3.3.4) Scalability
- The ability of a system to accomodate growth without become impractical or have peformance degradation.
• Mesh: growth increases the number of direct links rapidly.
• Bus: growth increases competition for a shared medium.
• Ring: growth may increase the number of intermediate nodes on a path.
• Star: growth increases dependence on the central device and its capacity.
• Tree: growth can be distributed across branches, although higher-level links remain important.


3.3.5) Which Topology is Used in Practice?
- No single topology is best for every network.
- The appropriate design depends on the network's size, traffic patterns, reliability requirements, costs, and communication architecture.

THUS: Network topology is a design choice. The objective is to provide the required communication path with balance and potential growth.

4. How Large is a Computer Network?
- Network scales enormously, from smallest to largest: PAN, LAN, MAN, and WAN.

4.1.1) Personal Area Network (PAN)
- Operates within a very small area, roughly "one square metre".
EX: A computer connected to a Bluetooth speaker.
NOTE: Focuses connections on nearby devices.


4.1.2) Local Area Network (LAN)
- Connecst to devices within an limited area from a room to a campus.
NOTE: Typically wired and focuses on local site. But can be wireless.


4.1.3) Metropolitan Area Network (MAN)
- Covers a city or metropolitan area, like cable TV.
NOTE: The medium may include coaxial cable, fiber-optic cable, or even wirless.


4.1.4) Wide Area Network (WAN)
- Covers an area larger than a city; countries, continents, or more.
NOTE: Provides connection across several LANs, rather than connecting every individual computer.
THUS: The communication across large geographical distances between multiple smaller networks.

5. Network Architectures and Models
- The topology a network uses, depends on the "model of communication", thus providing and consuming of resources.

5.1.1) The Client-Server Model
- A server provides a service or resource, while cliends request and use it.
RELATIONSHIP: Is asymmetric and hierarchical: The server provides, and the clients consume.

EX: Online banking. The bank operates a server, and many clients connect to it to carry out banking services.
- A centralized service allows the management of shared resources, apply security policies, and maintain authoritative information.
THUS: It models similarily to a star or tree topology, with the server occupying the central position. BUT completely different operations.

Constraints:
- The server may be overloaded when many clients make requests.
- Service becomes unavailable if a critical server or component fails.
- Service's capacity must grow to meet demand.
THUS: Congestion and Failure.



5.1.2) The Peer-To-Peer Model
- P2P distributes some of the work across participating computers, instead of one server to provide every piece of data to every user.
RELATIONSHIP: Equal and no hierarchy: Any given computer can become both a provider and a consumer, without a fixed role.

EX: BitTorrent
1) A computer downloads one portion of the file from another peer.
2) Once it has a portion available, it can provide that portion to other peers.
3) It may download different portions from several peers while uploading portions it already possesses.
4) The participating computers collectively contribute to distributing the file.

Constraints:
- Peers may join/leave at any time.
- Available resources and connection quality can vary.
- Coordination of peers to discover and locate data becomes complex.
- Data availablity depends on peer possession.
